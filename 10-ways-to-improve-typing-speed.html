10 Ways to Improve Typing Speed

Ten focused training methods for raising WPM by changing how you practice — not by repeating another generic list of typing tips.

Published: August 21, 2025 · Updated: September 14, 2026 · By Tibor Győry

Start with one rule: train the bottleneck, not the score

A typing test tells you the result, but it does not automatically tell you what to practice next. If you want to improve typing speed, first separate the skills behind your WPM: clean accuracy, repeated letter patterns, rhythm, recovery after errors, and endurance.

The methods below are designed as small training experiments. Pick one or two, use them for several sessions, and compare your results under the same test conditions.

1) Create a Repeatable Baseline

Improvement is difficult to see when every test is different. Start with three tests using the same duration and similar text difficulty. Record WPM and accuracy for all three, then use the average as your baseline instead of choosing the highest score.

This gives you a number that is harder to distort with one unusually easy or unusually difficult run. If you are unsure what the score represents, review how WPM is calculated.

Practice experiment: Take three 1-minute tests. Add the three WPM scores and divide by three. Repeat the same process one week later.

2) Find Your Controlled-Speed Zone

Your fastest possible pace is not always your best training pace. Do one normal test, then deliberately type a little slower on the next run. The goal is to find a pace where your rhythm feels comfortable and your accuracy stays consistently high.

That controlled pace becomes your training zone. From there, you can add speed gradually instead of repeatedly practicing at a pace where errors dominate the session.

Practice experiment: Compare one normal-speed run with one controlled run. If the second attempt loses only a little WPM but removes many corrections, use that pace for your main practice block.

3) Measure the Cost of Corrections

Two people can show the same WPM while having very different typing quality. One may type smoothly; the other may rush, backspace repeatedly, and recover from mistakes. Those corrections consume time and interrupt rhythm.

Instead of watching only WPM, notice how often a mistake forces you to stop. If errors are your main limitation, use the Typing Error Tracker and the dedicated Typing Accuracy Tips guide.

Practice experiment: For one test, count the corrections that noticeably interrupt your rhythm. Your next goal is not higher WPM — it is fewer interruptions at roughly the same pace.

4) Turn Repeated Errors into 2-Minute Micro-Drills

A repeated error is more useful than a random error because it tells you exactly what to train. Look for combinations that repeatedly slow you down: a particular letter pair, a word ending, punctuation, capitalization, or a transition between hands.

Extract the pattern and repeat it slowly for two minutes. Then return to normal text. This makes practice specific without turning the whole session into a long drill.

Example: If words containing tion repeatedly break your rhythm, practice short sequences such as “action, station, motion, section” before retesting.

5) Use Controlled Speed Intervals

Constant maximum-speed testing can make practice messy. A better speed session alternates controlled typing with short faster intervals. The faster block exposes you to a higher pace, while the controlled block prevents the whole session from becoming rushed.

Controlled2 minutesRecover accuracy and rhythm

Review1 minuteCheck whether errors increased

The faster interval should feel challenging, not uncontrolled. Stop increasing pace if accuracy collapses.

6) Train Endurance Separately from Peak WPM

A strong 30-second result does not guarantee that you can maintain the same quality for several minutes. If your speed falls during longer tests, endurance may be the real bottleneck.

Add one steady 3- to 5-minute run to selected sessions. Do not chase a record. Try to keep the first and last minute similar in rhythm and accuracy.

Practice experiment: Compare the first minute and final minute of a longer run. If the end becomes much slower or less accurate, train consistency before trying to raise peak WPM.

7) Increase Only One Difficulty Variable at a Time

Practice becomes hard to evaluate when you simultaneously type faster, use longer text, add punctuation, and extend the session. Change one variable and keep the others stable.

8) Practice Transfer to Real Text

WPM tests are useful for measurement, but everyday typing also includes punctuation, capitalization, longer sentences, editing, and thinking while you type. Once or twice per week, practice with realistic paragraphs instead of only short test words.

This is especially useful if your test WPM looks good but emails, notes, reports, or other real work still feels slow. The goal is to make your trained speed usable outside the test.

Practice experiment: Type a normal paragraph for three minutes without trying to set a record. Note where hesitation appears: punctuation, capitals, unfamiliar words, or corrections.

9) Use Weekly Averages Instead of Chasing Personal Records

Personal records are motivating, but they are poor progress indicators when used alone. Text difficulty, concentration, fatigue, and random variation can all move a single result.

A weekly average gives you a more stable trend. Record a small number of comparable tests and watch whether average WPM rises while accuracy remains stable.

Simple rule: Compare this week with last week — not your latest score with your all-time best. The Typing Progress Tracker can help keep the measurements together.

10) Run a Two-Week Improvement Cycle

Instead of changing your routine every day, choose one bottleneck and give it enough time to produce a measurable result. A simple two-week cycle keeps practice focused.

StageWhat to do
Day 1Record a three-test baseline and identify one bottleneck.
Days 2–6Use one targeted method from this page for 10–15 minutes.
Day 7Retest under the same conditions and review the trend.
Days 8–13Keep the method if it is helping; otherwise change one variable.
Day 14Retest and compare average WPM, accuracy, and repeated errors with Day 1.

FAQ: Training for Higher WPM

How should I measure typing-speed improvement?

Use the same test duration and similar text difficulty, record several results, and compare weekly average WPM and accuracy instead of only your best score.

How often should I do maximum-speed typing tests?

Use maximum-speed attempts sparingly. Most practice should stay controlled, with short faster intervals added only when accuracy remains reasonably stable.

What should I practice if my WPM is not improving?

Identify the limiting factor first. Repeated letter combinations, unstable accuracy, slow recovery after errors, poor endurance, or inconsistent rhythm each require different practice.

Is one long typing session better than short sessions?

Short focused sessions are easier to repeat consistently. Longer controlled runs are useful when endurance is specifically what you need to improve.
